Introduction

Character controllers can be extremely complex and can look very different depending on what the characters need to fulfill. There are many pre-made character controllers out in the world - Unity's “Starter assets: character controllers", Unreal's character controller asset, and many more that can be bought on different asset stores. What goes into making these controllers, and what makes a character controller good? I will be showcasing my findings on what goes into making a character controller and the process we in the team went through when working on this project.

This project is a prototype of a 3D platformer metroidvania, with inspiration from PlayStation 2-era platformers. We wanted the character movement and feel to be similar to the Sly series and Jack and Daxter series. The project was made in Unity 6, and I primarily worked with the game designer Carl Leong, and we had some help with graphics and audio from Hilda Kovács and Sol Forsberg.

What goes into making a character controller

First and foremost, a character controller needs to fill a purpose and fit the project. Now this can vary heavily from genre to mechanics and the style of the game. You don't want your character to feel like it’s jumping on the moon when it is inside a gravity chamber that has the gravitational pull 10x that of the Earth, and simultaneously the camera is somehow in front of you and is drifting off into space. That indeed would feel very weird, and the character controller will feel like it does not fit the scenario or the game. So what are the main things to think about before starting the process of making a character controller? A good design tool to use in this scenario is the 3Cs, aka Character, Camera, and Controller. How I would boil it down is the following.

Character

Character embodies the style, mechanics, and world of a game. A nimble, slim-looking ninja, having the speed and turn rate of an old riding lawn mower, would not feel like it's in character.

Camera

Camera is the eyes of the player; from a first-person perspective to a camera that is looking from the sky, and it also dictates how much control the player has over its own view.

Controller

Controller is all about the feeling of the character, its responsiveness, and the feedback of pressing buttons.

A great-feeling character controller needs to be able to do great in all of the 3C points.

When making the character controller, starting with figuring out the character of the game is very important; knowing what the game is going to be is needed to make a character controller that solves/fits the game. After figuring out the character, what the player is going to see comes next. Is the player able to see the whole world, or is it more narrowed? The camera will impact how the character is going to control, and how the logic needs to work in the game. Lastly, what will the player feel when controlling the character: tight responsiveness when making an input, or a built-in one-second input lag if you want to be really goofy.

When all of the points are covered, it makes the programming work way easier, and the programmer(me) can plan what is needed and how the character needs to work to cover the design.

The process of making our character controller

Before starting on any code, me and my co-developer(designer) wanted to make a 3D platformer metroidvania, in the style of a PS2-era game. We started by researching PS2- and PS3-era platformers, looking specifically at how the character controller felt to play and the mechanics that were used during that time. We were especially inspired by the Sly series and Jack and Daxter series. After we finalised the research, we had the mechanics that we wanted, and we had the feeling we wanted to have for the character controller. For mechanics, we wanted a dash, gliding, double jump, attacking, the ability to attach to specific objects, and time manipulation. For the feeling, we wanted it to be responsive and be forgiving while being smooth so it has a great flow to it. The camera was going to be a third-person perspective where the player could control where they were looking. To make the movement forgiving, we wanted there to be:

Coyote time

A small window where the player could jump even when they're not on a platform.

Mantle zone

Snapping the character on top of a platform even when they did not reach on top of it.

Jump buffer

A small window where the jump input gets stored when falling so the jump input doesn't need to be frame perfect, making it feel more responsive.

Variable jumping

Changing how high and long the player is in the air, making the player have more control over the jumping.

The process of making our character controller

When everything was said and done, we were able to implement most of the mechanics and get the feeling of the character controller down for the most parts. It was very helpful to look at Platformer Toolkit by Game Maker's Toolkit for getting a feeling for platforming tweaking and understanding how a character controller can work, and Math for Game Programmers: Building a Better Jump presented by Kyle Pittman to understand some of the math behind a good-feeling character controller.

The character controller was built with Unity's Character Controller components, which provide some built-in tools. Slopes, step-ups, and some movement functions are built into the component, making the start of the controller easier, but a lot needs to be built on top of the components to make it good.

Movement handling

Movement is handled by calculating a desired point that the character controller wants to move towards on the X and Z axes. The desired point is a direction and the max velocity the character can move. The character tries to follow the desired point by lerping towards its direction and controlling the character's velocity by how long the player holds the movement input. This gives great control over how fast we want the character to turn and how fast we want the character to accelerate. Other forces are added to the last calculation before using Unity’s Character Controller component Move function to move the character.

Slopes

Unity’s Character Controller component can handle slope detection and the ability to walk on them, but there are some problems with it. If the character can jump or walk off a ledge and land on a slope that surpasses the slope angle limit, the character doesn't fall off. Which is reasonable because the components don't have physics and can't slide off stuff. To fix this, sliding needs to be implemented. First, the character needs to check if it is on an illegal slope; this is done by calculating the angle with the ground normal. If the angle is over the slope limit, it’s illegal to walk on, and the character should start sliding.

Step-up

Unity’s Character Controller component has step-up functionality built in, but it’s not all that reliable and, most importantly, not always controllable. So I implemented my own step up functionality. It worked by raycasting in front of the character to detect if there are any objects that are plausible to step up to. If an object is detected, another raycast is used to detect if it hits on top of the object, to get a coordinate to teleport to.

Pushing object

In the game, there are objects that can give forces to the player to make the controller move in a specific direction or move with an object. This can be done by forcefully moving the object's collider away from each other, but this can look extremely choppy and bad. To make this smoother, the character checks the object under it and gets its movement velocity. And applies it to the character controller. This worked for most cases, but it has some problems when objects try to push the character upwards, where some choppiness can happen, especially at low velocities.

Time manipulation

The time manipulation we wanted for the game was to be able to control nearly all entities' update speed, except the player, which was immune to this effect. The time manipulation is quite simple. It works by multiplying a time value by a float value to manipulate how much the time value should affect movement and timers in the game. Every object that should be affected by this manipulated time value uses it when doing its calculations.

Mistakes along the way

The project time we had was not long, and we in the project wanted to make a prototype of a game. When beginning the project, I had budgeted the character controller to take 3-4 weeks, including all the mechanics and having the movement we wanted. This was a great underestimate on my part; it took closer to six weeks to complete the character controller to a satisfying degree. I also needed to rewrite how the gravity and movement worked three weeks in because it was hard to work with and not tweakable enough for my designer to work with.

In hindsight, I also believe that I chose the wrong physics component to work with in this project. Starting out, I thought that we didn't want a lot of physics objects and the character controller would have a stylized gravity, so I didn't use Unity’s Rigidbody component. When mechanics and objects started to need physics, this became a problem. The biggest problem we had with this was mostly when objects needed to push the character controller. When the character controller started to get pushed, a lot of jittering was happening on the character and with the camera, making it look quite bad. In the end, I was able to come up with a half fix for it, but the problem is still in the game. With a Rigidbody, this could be handled with its interpolation. We also didn’t benefit a lot from Unity's Character Controller component, when we needed to overwrite its inbuilt features and implement a lot of our own stuff.

Camera

The current camera that the character controller uses is a Cinemachine camera with an orbital follow and a deoccluder; the camera follows the player orbitally and has hindrance detection, so it moves the camera to an available space. This works good enough but comes with some problems. We had a problem where the camera was shaking when walking around. It took a lot of time to debug and fix. Why I’m not satisfied with the camera is mostly because I was not the one who made it. Cinemachine is a prebuilt package that has a lot of good camera tools, but using it comes with the cost of losing some control over how the camera will work and how to tweak it. I wanted to make my own custom camera, but it was so late in development that I didn't feel like I had the time and knowledge to make it good.

I believe we overscoped the project for the experience level and the amount of time we had, forcing us to cut some of the things we wanted to do. We wanted there to be more of an emphasis on the metroidvania part in the game, but because of time constraints, we couldn't implement those features.

What I learned

I learned a hell of a lot about character controllers and the complexities of creating one, and the amount of detailed work that is needed to make it good. I got better at doing necessary research to get a better grasp of a work area, and a greater understanding of what in particular makes a character controller feel good in a platformer.
